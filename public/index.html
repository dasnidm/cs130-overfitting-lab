UNDER THE HOOD

Choices become sales.
Sales become data.

The same lesson matters for HUMANA: evaluate a forecast on later campaigns before relying on it for planning.

How are customers and items generated?

Each campaign begins with 120 items and 180 potential customers. Independently draw item attractiveness A and each customer’s minimum acceptable score θ from Normal(5.5, 1.5²), rejecting values outside [1, 10], then round to integers. This makes 5 and 6 the most likely scores. Independently draw a fixed waiting weight w ~ Beta(2, 2) for each customer.

Each customer first arrives on a uniformly random sale day. Shuffle the active customers each day. A customer considers only items with A ≥ θ and, when buying, takes the highest score available. Everyone can afford €6 and buys at most one item. Waiting shoppers return daily; shoppers with no acceptable item leave permanently.

How does a customer decide to buy or wait?

Let S be the number of remaining acceptable items at that customer’s turn. The assumed perceived risk of finding none tomorrow is q = 10 / (10 + S). It rises as eligible stock runs out. This is a chosen scarcity rule, not a calibrated stockout probability.

Tomorrow’s proportional saving is s = (pt − pt+1) / pt. Buy now when (1 − w)q ≥ ws; otherwise wait. On the final day, buy if any acceptable item remains. Every purchase immediately removes one item from stock.

How do those choices become regression data?

One observation is one complete campaign. Independently draw its final markdown d uniformly from 10% to 80%. For T days, set pt = 6[1 − (d/100)(t − 1)/(T − 1)]. Prices are calculated without cent rounding. The chosen duration is the same across all campaigns in one experiment. Potential customers stay fixed at 180 even when duration changes.

Daily sales Qt come from the purchase rules above; total revenue is R = Σt=1T ptQt. Fresh items, customers, arrival days, and queue order generate variation. There is no preset revenue curve or added revenue noise.

The fitted polynomial predicts total revenue from d. All 2,000 test campaigns use fresh people and stock, and none enters the fit. RMSE is √[Σ(R − R̂)² / n]. The separate example above uses d = 66⅔%, so five days gives €6 → €5 → €4 → €3 → €2. It is illustrative, not a record of a HUMANA sale.

What does the overfitting comparison show?

Unregularized ordinary least squares polynomials of degrees 1–12 are nested: adding terms cannot increase training RMSE on fixed data, apart from rounding. More flexible fits can follow random customer variation and perform worse on new campaigns. A quadratic is only an approximation here; the generator is a sequence of customer decisions.

The 50-sample view averages RMSE across 50 fresh training/test pairs. The fit and example panels continue to show the first sample. A U-shape is not guaranteed for every setting. Changing duration changes both daily markdowns and arrival timing. Selecting a model after inspecting the test curve requires another untouched test set for a final evaluation.

The assumed shared attractiveness ranking, budgets, arrival process, and risk rule have not been checked against HUMANA data. The app demonstrates model evaluation; its forecasts cannot establish a real optimal promotion.
